01 / Capital Investment ↓ $500B–$1T

The investment boom that won't happen

In the two years after the IRA passed, U.S. clean energy investment surged 71% — from $288B to $493B — as manufacturers raced to claim manufacturing tax credits and build domestic supply chains. Goldman Sachs projected ~$290B/year through 2032. The OBBBA terminated or curtailed the credits driving that investment. In 2025, the sector recorded its first net capital outflow since 2022: $34.8B in cancellations against $12.3B in new announcements.

Notable cancellations in 2025: GM's $4.3B Orion EV plant (Michigan), Freyr Battery's $2.6B Georgia factory, Kore Power's $1.2B Arizona battery facility. ~$19.9B in losses fell in Republican-held congressional districts — the same districts that received ~85% of all IRA investment.

02 / Employment ↓ 760,000 jobs by 2030

Three years of job creation, then a cliff

From 2022 through 2024, the IRA directly drove 42,000 new manufacturing jobs in solar, wind, and batteries annually — while overall U.S. manufacturing shed 55,000 jobs in the same period. These weren't minimum-wage service jobs: IRA credits required projects to pay prevailing wages and use registered apprenticeships to receive full credit value (a 5x multiplier), directly incentivizing high-quality employment. Unionization in clean energy hit a record 12.4% in 2023 — the first time it surpassed the broader energy sector average. Energy Innovation models a 760,000-job loss vs. the IRA trajectory by 2030.

03 / Consumer & Industrial Costs ↑ $1,100/yr household swing

Cheap energy was within reach

As new clean capacity lowered wholesale prices, the IRA trajectory pointed toward households saving $730–$1,135/year by 2035. The OBBBA flips that to a projected $78–$192/year increase. Wholesale electricity prices — which set the floor for industrial and consumer bills — are projected to rise 74% above the IRA baseline by 2035, adding $7–11B/year to industrial energy costs and eroding U.S. manufacturing competitiveness.

04 / Power Generation Capacity ↓ 340 GW by 2035

The grid that won't get built

2024 was a record year — 30+ GW of utility-scale solar and 10.3 GW of battery storage added, with solar accounting for 58% of all new U.S. electricity capacity. Princeton's REPEAT Project projected that trajectory accelerating to ~100 GW/year of combined additions by 2030. The OBBBA — by eliminating wind and solar credits for projects after 2027 — cuts that buildout 53–59%.

05 / The Other Side What proponents argued

The case made for the OBBBA

Four arguments made by OBBBA supporters deserve honest evaluation. Each is presented alongside what independent data and economic modeling actually shows. Where evidence supports or is silent on a claim it is marked as such; where modeling contradicts it, that is noted too.

Argument 1 — Fiscal
IRA credits were ballooning out of control

JCT's cost estimate for IRA energy credits grew from $391B (2022) to a projected $870B–$1.1T as utilization surpassed forecasts. Proponents argued the open-ended, uncapped credits represented uncontrolled mandatory spending. The OBBBA eliminates ~$496–$540B of that over 10 years.

The cost escalation is real and documented. However, the economic activity those credits generated — investment, jobs, tax revenue — partially offsets the fiscal savings. CBO does not model the dynamic multiplier effects of canceling investment incentives mid-cycle.
Argument 2 — National Security
Stop Chinese firms from claiming U.S. tax credits

The IRA had FEOC (Foreign Entity of Concern) restrictions only on EV credits. The OBBBA extended FEOC bars to six additional credits, preventing companies with material ties to China, Russia, North Korea, or Iran from claiming U.S. manufacturing and power generation subsidies. This provision has broad bipartisan support.

Independent analysts broadly agree FEOC restrictions are legitimate national security policy. The Bipartisan Policy Center found "broad bipartisan support for increasing domestic manufacturing and reducing reliance on Chinese manufacturing." The FEOC expansion is the most defensible provision in the OBBBA on the evidence.
Argument 3 — Preserved & Expanded
The OBBBA didn't kill all clean energy policy

Several credits were preserved or expanded: nuclear power credits unchanged; geothermal credits intact; Section 45Q carbon capture rates increased; Section 45Z clean fuels extended through 2029; 100% bonus depreciation made permanent (broadly pro-investment). Proponents argue this represents a technology-neutral pivot away from mandating wind and solar.

Accurate as stated. Nuclear and carbon capture receive continued support. However, the preserved credits are far smaller in scale: wind and solar represented the vast majority of the IRA's capacity-building effect. The CGEP at Columbia notes the preserved portfolio cannot replace the foregone buildout pace.
Argument 4 — Macroeconomic
The full bill boosts GDP through tax cuts

Tax Foundation projects the OBBBA's full package — primarily the TCJA extension, 100% bonus depreciation, and other business tax cuts — raises long-run GDP by +0.7% and provides a short-run boost of +0.4% to +1.2% (2025–2028). The bill is presented as a net economic positive.

The GDP boost comes from the tax cut provisions, not from repealing clean energy credits. Tax Foundation, Rhodium, and Energy Innovation all find the energy provisions specifically are a net GDP negative ($980B–$1.1T drag). The Tax Policy Center separately notes the overall bill "increases debt while modestly boosting the economy" — meaning the growth is deficit-financed.
